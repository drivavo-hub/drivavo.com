How you know it's time to step back

The signs show up long before the decision does.

Bill Henry · October 8, 2026

Nobody wakes up one morning and decides to sell. It comes on slowly. Most owners I talk to noticed the signs a year or two before they said anything out loud, and some of them noticed and then went quiet about it for a good while longer. Here are the ones I hear most.

The phone. You used to grab it on the first ring. A call meant a job, and a job meant money. Now you look at the screen and let it go. Not because you're busy. Because you already know what it is. A truck is down, a customer is unhappy, a tech didn't show. You'll call back in an hour. You used to call back in a minute.

The son who said no. Or the daughter, or the nephew, or the foreman you'd been bringing along for years. You asked, maybe not in so many words, and the answer was no. He moved out of state. She has her own career and likes it. He loves you and doesn't want your life. That's a hard one to hear, and most owners hear it and then don't talk about it for a long time.

The truck. There's a truck in the yard that should have been replaced two years ago. You know what it would cost. You could afford it. You didn't buy it, and if you're honest, you didn't buy it because somewhere in the back of your head you were thinking, why put that kind of money into something I won't be running in five years. That math is the decision, made before you'd admit you'd made it.

The quiet ones

Some signs are smaller and easier to miss.

You stopped hiring ahead of the work. You used to add a man before you needed him because you knew the calls were coming. Now you wait until the crew is underwater, and some weeks you let a job go rather than add a body.

The schedule lives in your head and nowhere else. If you took two weeks off, the trucks would roll, but by day four someone would be calling you. You've known this for years. You haven't fixed it because fixing it is work, and you have less of that in you than you used to.

The person at home has stopped asking when you're going to slow down. They didn't give up. They already know the answer. They're waiting for you to catch up to it.

And you've started doing the math. Not in a spreadsheet. In your head, late, on the building and the trucks and what's in receivables. What it would all come to if it were somebody else's to run.

What the signs mean

None of this means you have to sell. Plenty of owners feel every one of these and keep going for another ten years, and that's a fine choice. But the signs are telling you something, and what they're telling you is that the business has turned into a job you'd rather not have. A business the owner would rather not run gets a little less valuable every year he keeps running it. The customers can tell. The crew can tell. The truck doesn't get replaced, and then the next one doesn't either.

What the signs don't mean is that something is wrong with you. I spent over twenty years building a company from a handful of people to well over a hundred, and I still own it. I know exactly what it feels like to dread the phone. The day you admit it isn't a failure. It's the day you start making the decision on purpose instead of by default.

I buy owner-run service businesses, the kind that have been around ten years or more and have customers who come back every month or every season. I buy with my own money, not a fund. I'm the owner, not the operator. The people who run the day keep running it.

If you're reading this and seeing yourself in it, you don't have to do anything today. You don't need a broker, a valuation, or a plan. The first step is a conversation, and it can be a quiet one.
