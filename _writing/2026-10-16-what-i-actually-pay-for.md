When an owner tells me what his business is worth, he usually starts with the wrong list. The building. The trucks. The name on the side of them. Thirty years of reputation. All of that is real, and I don't dismiss any of it. But it isn't what I'm paying for, and if you understand what a buyer like me is paying for, you'll understand why two shops with the same revenue can get very different offers.

Here is the list I use.

Customers who come back without being asked. A pool route, a quarterly pest contract, a septic customer who gets pumped on a schedule and calls you when the alarm goes off. That's the center of the whole thing. If I can look at a year of invoices and see the same names showing up month after month, I know the revenue will be there next year whether or not anybody sells anything. Project work is fine, and most shops have some. But a dollar that comes back on its own is worth more to me than a dollar you had to go win again, and I pay for it that way.

A crew that stays. I want to see the same names on the payroll three years running. When the techs have been there five, eight, twelve years, that tells me the customers know them, the work gets done the same way every time, and nobody is walking out the week after closing. Turnover is a cost the buyer eats, and I price for it. A stable crew is money in the owner's pocket, even if he has never thought of it that way.

A second person who runs the day. This is the one most owners underestimate. If there is someone, an office manager, a lead tech, a foreman, who already builds the schedule, orders the parts, and takes the angry call when you're out, the business is worth more. Not a little more. A lot more. That person is the reason the business keeps working after you leave. If there isn't one, I can put a manager in, and I will. But I pay for that out of the price, and the owner who already has that person in place gets paid for it instead.

Clean-enough numbers. Not perfect. Clean enough. Revenue that ties to bank deposits. Payroll that matches the people I'd meet in the shop. Tax returns that look like the P&L, or at least rhyme with it. I'll write about messy books another week, because I can work around a lot. But the closer the numbers are to the truth, the more I can pay, because the less I have to hold back for what I might find later.

## What I don't pay for

The owner's name. If every customer calls because they want you, personally, and would not take the call from anyone else, then what you have is a reputation, not a business, and a reputation doesn't transfer. The good news is that most owners overestimate how much of this is true. The customers like you. They also like the tech who has been showing up at their house for six years. Where it is true, it means we plan a longer handoff. It doesn't mean the deal is dead.

The shop building. If you own the real estate, it has value, but it's a separate deal. I'll usually rent it from you, which gives you a check every month after the sale, and we price the business as a business. Folding the building into the price muddies both numbers and usually leaves the owner worse off on one of them.

The potential. Every owner has a story about what the business could do with a second truck, a bigger crew, a push into the next county. I believe you. But I pay for what the business does, not what it could do. If the potential were already cash, you would have it. If it isn't, I'm the one taking the risk to get there. That isn't me being hard. It's me being honest about who is doing the work after closing.

## What this means for you

If you're a few years out, this list is also a to-do list. Get the recurring work on paper. Keep the crew. Give one person more of the day, and let them have it. Get the numbers to tie. None of that costs much. All of it shows up when a buyer sits down across from you.

I buy owner-run service businesses with my own money, not a fund. I'm the owner, not the operator. The people who run the day keep running it.
