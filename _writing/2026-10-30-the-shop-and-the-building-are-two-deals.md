A lot of the owners I talk to own two things and think of them as one. There's the business, and there's the building the business runs out of. Years ago you bought the building because rent felt like money out the door, and you were right. Now you're thinking about selling, and the building comes up in the first ten minutes.

Here is how I think about it. The shop and the building are two different things for sale, with two different prices, and sometimes two different buyers. Once you see them that way, you have three choices instead of one.

## Three ways to do it

Sell both. You sell me the business and the building at the same closing, and you walk away with cash for each. It's the cleanest break. No lease to manage, no roof to worry about, no relationship with the new owner after the handoff. The trade is that nothing comes in afterward. You get paid once.

Sell the business, keep the building. You sell me the business, and we sign a lease with you as the landlord. Every month the business pays you rent, the same way it paid you before, except now someone else runs the shop and you collect. For a lot of owners this is the best of both. You get paid for the business at closing, and you keep an asset that pays you every month. The thing to understand is that your tenant is the business you just sold. If it does well, the rent shows up. That's one more reason I care about the crew and the manager staying. It's your check too.

Sell the building, keep the business. This is the one most owners don't know they have. You sell the building, keep running the business, and lease the space back. The building turns into cash now, and the business keeps its address, its phone number and its routine. Nothing changes for your crew or your customers. The trade runs the other way from the second path: now you write the rent check every month instead of receiving it. Owners pick this when they want to pull money out of the real estate without selling the company yet, or when they plan to sell the business later and want the two kept apart.

I'm a buyer on all three paths. I buy service businesses and I buy small industrial buildings, so I can take either half or both.

## Why I price them separately

The business is worth what it earns. The building is worth the rent it collects. Those are different math, and mixing them usually hurts the owner on at least one side.

Where it goes wrong is rent. A lot of owners who own their building pay themselves very little rent, or none. That makes the business look more profitable than it is, because it isn't carrying the cost of its space. When I price the business, the first thing I do is put a fair rent on it, the rent a stranger would pay for that building. Then I price what's left. That isn't me shaving the number. If you keep the building, that fair rent is exactly what you collect, every month. If you sell me the building, a fair lease already in place is part of what the building is worth.

The lease matters more than owners expect. How long it runs, how the rent goes up, who pays the taxes, the insurance and the repairs. A fair lease with real term on it protects both sides, and it's usually the first thing I'd put on paper.

## What to decide first

Before you talk to any buyer, decide which check you want after closing: one big one, a monthly one coming in, or cash now with a monthly one going out. Then talk to your accountant, because the three paths are not taxed the same, and that difference can matter as much as the price.

You don't have to settle all of it on the first call. But know going in that the building is a second deal, not a footnote to the first one.
